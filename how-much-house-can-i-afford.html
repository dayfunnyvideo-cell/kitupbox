How Much House Can I Afford?

5 min read · Nov 26, 2026

Before you look at listings, find out what monthly payment fits your life. The price on the listing is only part of the cost. This guide shows a simple way to estimate what you can afford, with a worked example you can copy.

Start with the monthly payment, not the price

Most people think about a house as one big number. A lender thinks about the monthly payment. A home can look cheap and still cost too much each month once taxes, insurance and interest are added. So begin with what you can pay each month, then work backward to a price.

What is in a monthly mortgage payment

  • Principal and interest (P&I): the loan itself and the interest on it.
  • Property tax: varies a lot by state and county.
  • Homeowners insurance: protects the house. Lenders require it.
  • HOA fees: only if the home is in a homeowners association.
  • PMI (private mortgage insurance): usually added when your down payment is under 20%.

The 28/36 rule

A common guideline used in the US is the 28/36 rule. It is a rule of thumb, not a law. Lenders have their own limits, and they can be higher or lower.

  • 28%: your housing costs (payment, taxes, insurance, HOA) should be about 28% of your gross monthly income or less.
  • 36%: all your monthly debt payments together, including the house, should be about 36% of your gross monthly income or less.
Housing limit = gross monthly income × 0.28
Total debt limit = gross monthly income × 0.36

A worked example

Income: $90,000 a year, which is $7,500 a month before tax.
Housing limit (28%): $7,500 × 0.28 = $2,100 a month.
Total debt limit (36%): $7,500 × 0.36 = $2,700 a month.

Now test a home. Say the price is $300,000 and you put 20% down ($60,000). You borrow $240,000 for 30 years at 6.5% interest. The principal and interest payment is about $1,517. Add about $375 a month for taxes and insurance, and the total is about $1,892.

ItemMonthly
Principal and interest$1,517
Taxes and insurance (example)$375
Total housing$1,892
Your 28% limit$2,100

$1,892 is under $2,100, so this home fits the 28% rule. Next, check the 36% rule. Your total debt limit is $2,700. After the house, that leaves $2,700 − $1,892 = $808 for car loans, student loans and credit card minimums. If your other debts are higher than that, you can afford a cheaper home, not this one.

How each factor changes your budget

  • Higher interest rate: the same price costs more every month.
  • Bigger down payment: a smaller loan and lower payment. At 20% or more you usually avoid PMI.
  • Existing debt: every dollar of other debt takes away from what you can spend on a home.
  • Local taxes and insurance: the same price can cost very different amounts in different states.

Costs people forget

  • Closing costs: fees paid when you buy. Ask your lender for an estimate.
  • Repairs and upkeep: the roof, the heater and the water heater do not last forever.
  • Utilities: larger homes cost more to heat and cool.
  • Moving and furniture.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need 20% down to buy a house?

No. Many loans accept less. But with under 20% down you usually pay PMI, which adds to the monthly cost.

Is the 28/36 rule a must?

No. It is a guideline. Some lenders approve higher ratios, but a higher ratio leaves less room if your costs rise.

Does a lender's approval amount equal what I can afford?

Not always. A lender tells you the most they will lend. Your own budget, savings and comfort decide what is right for you.
