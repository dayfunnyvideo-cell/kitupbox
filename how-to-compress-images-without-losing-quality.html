How to Compress Images Without Losing Quality

Large image files can slow down your website, fill up your email inbox and take up too much space on your phone. But compressing an image the wrong way can make it blurry or blocky. The good news is that you can usually make a photo much smaller and still not see any difference with your eyes. This guide shows how.

Can you compress an image with no quality loss?

There are two kinds of compression. Lossless compression shrinks the file without throwing away any picture data, so the image is exactly the same, but the savings are usually small. Lossy compression removes details the eye hardly notices, so the file gets much smaller. JPG photos use lossy compression.

So "without losing quality" really means "without losing quality you can see". At a sensible setting, most people cannot tell a compressed JPG from the original, even though the file may be a fraction of the size.

Why do images lose quality?

Quality drops when the compression is too strong or is applied again and again. Every time you save a JPG at a lower quality, a little more detail is thrown away, and the damage adds up. It is most visible in images with fine detail, sharp text or smooth color gradients. This is why it is best to compress once, from the original file.

Resize first, then compress

Many photos are far bigger than they need to be. A phone photo can be 4,000 pixels wide, while a picture on a web page often only needs to be about 1,200 to 1,600 pixels wide. If you shrink the dimensions first, the file gets much smaller before compression even starts. You can do that with our Image Resizer. Then run the smaller image through the compressor.

Choose the right format

  • JPG: best for photographs. It gives small files and works everywhere.
  • PNG: best for logos, screenshots, graphics with sharp edges and anything that needs a transparent background. PNG files are larger than JPG for photos.
  • WebP: a newer format that is often smaller than JPG or PNG at similar quality. Most modern browsers support it, but older programs may not open it.

Tips for the best image quality

  • Use the right format: JPG for photographs and PNG for graphics with transparency.
  • Do not shrink too much: if you need a small image, start from a high-resolution original rather than enlarging a small one.
  • Compress once: always work from the original file, not from an image that was already compressed.
  • Keep a backup: save the original uncompressed file in case you need it later.
  • Check the result: look at the image at the size people will see it, not only zoomed in.

Common mistakes

  • Setting the quality too low, so that blocky patches appear around edges.
  • Compressing a screenshot or text image as a JPG, which can make the text look fuzzy. Use PNG for those.
  • Saving the same JPG again and again.
  • Uploading a huge photo to a website and letting the page shrink it, which still makes visitors download the large file.

Frequently asked questions

What is the ideal quality setting?

For websites, 75% to 80% is a good balance. For printing, use 90% or higher.

Will compressing an image make it blurry?

Not at a sensible setting. Blurriness or blocky patches show up when the quality is set too low or the image is compressed several times.

Can I compress multiple images at once?

Our free tool currently processes one image at a time.

Is there a file size limit?

We recommend files under 10 MB for the best performance.

How small should an image be to attach to an email?

Email services limit the total size of attachments, so smaller files are safer. Compressing photos before you send them helps them go through and load faster for the person who receives them.
